Study journey · La Paz to Heidelberg

A winding route into computational neuroimaging

The route from electronic engineering to brain imaging was built across several fields: control systems, mathematics, software, and biomedical engineering.

My formal training began with electronic engineering at Universidad Mayor de San Andrés in La Paz. Alongside it, I studied undergraduate mathematics. The engineering work included control systems, and my bachelor thesis focused on reconstructing rotations with quaternions and an optimal observer. It was an early encounter with a question that would keep returning: how should a computational method respect the structure of the thing it represents?

After working in software engineering and research, I moved to Heidelberg for a master's in biomedical engineering. That shift brought computation closer to clinical and biological data. Projects ranged from gait-phase classification and ultrasound signal reconstruction to medical image analysis.

Finding structure in images

The master's thesis made the connection concrete. Lung vessels form branching, connected structures, while a conventional image grid treats every location as if it were independent of its neighbours. The thesis explored geometric deep learning for vessel segmentation in CT, using a graph representation to bring connectivity into the model.

This work became a bridge between engineering and neuroimaging. It led toward questions about learning on surfaces and shapes: what information is carried by anatomy itself, and how can a model learn from it without flattening away its geometry?

From medical engineering to brain shape

In 2021, I began doctoral research at Leipzig University and the Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences. The focus is geometric deep learning for neuroimaging, including hippocampal shape analysis in ageing and neurodegenerative disease.

Looking back across these stages, the path is less a straight line than a gradual narrowing of the question. Control systems, medical images, meshes and brain anatomy all involve structured data. Learning to notice that structure has become a useful compass for choosing both research problems and methods.
