Study journey · Master's thesis, 2019

Learning on the shape of a problem

A lung-vessel segmentation project showed how the geometry of medical data can guide the design of a learning system.

For my master's thesis in biomedical engineering at Heidelberg University, I worked on segmenting lung vessels in CT. The goal was to identify vessel structures in volumetric scans, where fine branches and connections matter to the anatomy being measured.

A regular image grid is a convenient representation, but it does not automatically encode the fact that vessels form a connected network. The thesis explored a geometric fully convolutional network that used this connected structure as part of the representation. In other words, the model was designed around a property of the anatomy instead of asking the network to infer every relationship from pixel coordinates alone.

Representation is part of the method

That distinction is central to geometric deep learning. Data can live on grids, graphs, surfaces or other structured spaces, and the choice affects which relationships are easy for a model to express. A graph representation makes adjacency explicit; a surface mesh carries local geometry and connectivity; a point cloud retains coordinates without a fixed grid.

The thesis evaluated the approach on VESSEL12 and reported state-of-the-art results for the task at the time. More lasting than any single score was the practical lesson: before selecting a network, make clear what the data's structure is and which parts of it the task depends on.

A question that carried forward

Later work on brain surfaces and hippocampal shape has continued this line of thinking. A vessel tree and a hippocampal mesh are different objects, but both make geometry relevant to the learning problem. The thesis gave me a first sustained opportunity to explore that connection in medical imaging.
